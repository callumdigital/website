The problem

Adding a second major works differently depending on whether it sits inside your programme or comes from another one. Students didn't think in those terms.

What I did

Ran guerrilla intercept interviews to learn what students actually understood, then designed the choice around that.

The result

The final UI asks students what they already know, instead of making them decode one long dropdown. [ADD A NUMBER IF YOU HAVE ONE]

Jargon, decoded

Programme
The degree you're enrolled in, like a Bachelor of Arts.
Major
The main subject you specialise in within that degree.
Second major, inside your programme
Another major from the same degree. [CHECK WORDING]
Second major, from another programme
A major that belongs to a different degree. [CHECK WORDING + ANY RULES WORTH NAMING]

The problem

There are two ways to add a second major: from inside your programme, or from another programme. Each one follows different rules, and the system needed students to pick the right path.

[ONE REAL MOMENT WHERE A STUDENT GOT THIS WRONG, AND WHAT IT COST THEM]

What we found

I ran guerrilla intercept interviews: grabbing students on campus for a few minutes and asking what they understood about majors and programmes. 39 students in total, 23 future and 16 current.

  1. [INSIGHT 1]: [ONE LINE, IDEALLY A QUOTE]
  2. [INSIGHT 2]: [ONE LINE]
  3. Design around what they know. Instead of teaching students our terms, the flow should start from the things they already understand.

The call

The BA proposed one aggregated dropdown with every major in it, each labelled [inside] or [outside].

I argued it would confuse people: once every option carries a label, students have to understand the label before they can pick anything. The research said they didn't. Selections based on what students already know won, and that shaped the final UI.
